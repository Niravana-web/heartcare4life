## What is a treadmill stress test?

A treadmill stress test records your heart while you walk. Your cardiologist can then see how it responds to physical effort. Sticky electrodes on your chest connect to an electrocardiogram machine. That machine traces your heart's electrical activity continuously. A cuff checks your blood pressure every few minutes. You start walking slowly on a level treadmill. Every three minutes the belt speeds up and the incline rises. The goal is to reach a target heart rate for your age. You may instead reach the point where symptoms or fatigue stop you. Many heart problems stay hidden at rest. They only appear when the heart has to work harder. This test brings them out. It also measures your exercise capacity and blood pressure response. It shows whether symptoms like chest tightness match what the tracing shows. It is also called an exercise stress test or a cardiac stress test.

The whole appointment usually runs about an hour. Roughly ten to fifteen minutes of that is on the treadmill.

## Why Dr. Nanavati may recommend this

The main question is whether the heart gets enough blood during exertion. Reduced flow suggests [coronary artery disease](/conditions/coronary-artery-disease). That is the narrowing of heart arteries by plaque. The [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](https://www.cdc.gov/heart-disease/) reports that heart disease remains the leading cause of death in the United States.

Dr. Nanavati may order a treadmill test when:

- You have [chest pain](/conditions/chest-pain), pressure, or unusual shortness of breath with activity.
- Your risk factors warrant a closer look, such as diabetes, smoking, or a strong family history.
- Symptoms such as palpitations or lightheadedness appear during exercise.
- Your exercise capacity needs measuring before starting a program or returning to work.
- A known heart condition is being followed over time.
- Rhythm changes during exertion need to be documented.

A resting [electrocardiogram](/services/electrocardiogram-ecg-ekg) is normal in many people with narrowed arteries. That is exactly why exercise testing exists.

## How to prepare

- Do not eat a heavy meal for about three to four hours beforehand. A light snack is usually acceptable. Confirm with the office.
- Avoid caffeine and nicotine on the day of the test.
- Ask which medicines to hold. Beta blockers slow the heart rate. They can prevent you from reaching the target. Never stop one without instruction.
- Wear loose clothing and, most importantly, proper walking shoes. Sandals and dress shoes make the test harder than it needs to be.
- Skip lotions, oils, and powders on your chest. Electrodes will not stick to them.
- Expect that a small area of chest hair may be clipped so the electrodes hold.
- Bring your inhaler if you use one. Bring your medication list too.
- Allow about one hour at the office.

## What happens during the procedure

You change into a gown that opens at the front. Ten electrodes are placed on your chest. A blood pressure cuff goes on your arm. A resting tracing and a resting blood pressure are recorded first. This is done standing and lying down.

The treadmill starts slowly. Most people describe the pace as an easy stroll. Every three minutes it becomes faster and steeper. Staff talk with you throughout. They ask how hard you feel you are working. Hold the handrails lightly for balance rather than leaning on them. Leaning reduces the workload and can affect results.

The test stops when you reach your target heart rate. It also stops when you feel you cannot continue. Staff will stop it if they see a reason to. Fatigue and leg tiredness are the most common reasons. Stopping for those is normal, not a failure. Tell staff immediately about chest pain, severe breathlessness, dizziness, or leg pain.

Afterward you slow to a cool-down walk. You then sit or lie down. Monitoring continues for several minutes into recovery. Some meaningful changes appear only after exercise stops.

## Recovery and results

There is no downtime. You can eat, drive, and return to normal activity right away. Many people feel pleasantly tired for a few hours.

Your cardiologist reviews the ECG tracings at each stage. Your blood pressure response and how long you exercised are checked next. So are the heart rate you reached and any symptoms you reported. A report is usually ready within a few business days. Dr. Nanavati will then discuss it with you.

A normal result is genuinely reassuring. It often shifts the focus to prevention, cholesterol control, and exercise. An abnormal or unclear result usually leads to imaging. That may be a [nuclear stress test](/services/nuclear-stress-test) or another form of [advanced stress testing](/services/advanced-stress-testing). It sometimes leads to an angiogram. You may already have known heart disease. The results may then guide an exercise prescription through [cardiac rehabilitation](/services/cardiac-rehabilitation).

## Risks and safety

Exercise testing is safe for the great majority of patients. Trained staff and emergency equipment are present throughout. Serious complications are uncommon. Possible effects include:

- Fatigue, leg heaviness, or breathlessness during and shortly after the test.
- Chest discomfort, which is important information rather than a setback.
- Dizziness or a drop in blood pressure.
- Irregular heartbeats, usually brief.
- Skin irritation from the electrode adhesive.
- Very rarely, a heart attack or a serious rhythm problem.

Tell the staff about anything you feel. Stopping early is always allowed. The [National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/heart-tests) explains how stress testing is supervised and what the results can show.
