## What is pacemaker interrogation?

Pacemaker interrogation is a painless check of an implanted pacemaker. A handheld wand or wireless programmer is held over your chest, and it communicates directly with the device under your skin. Nothing is inserted, and no needles are used. The check reads three things. First, how much battery life remains. Second, whether the leads, the thin wires connecting the device to your heart, are still working properly. Third, what rhythms the pacemaker has recorded since your last visit, including any fast or slow episodes you may not have felt. Your cardiologist can also adjust settings during the same visit, such as the lowest heart rate the device will allow. The appointment usually takes fifteen to thirty minutes, and you can drive yourself home afterward. Interrogation is how a pacemaker stays matched to your needs as your health changes over the years.

Many modern devices also send data from home through a bedside or smartphone transmitter, which reduces the number of in-office visits needed.

## Why Dr. Nanavati may recommend this

Every pacemaker needs regular checks. A pacemaker treats slow or unreliable heart rhythms by sending gentle electrical signals to the heart, and the [National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/pacemakers) describes how these devices are monitored over their lifetime.

Interrogation is done:

- On a routine schedule, typically every three to twelve months depending on your device
- Shortly after implantation, to confirm the settings suit you
- When you have dizziness, fainting, fatigue, breathlessness, or [heart palpitations](/conditions/heart-palpitations)
- If a lead or generator problem is suspected
- After a hospital stay, a heart procedure, or a change in your heart medicines
- Before and after certain scans or surgeries, including MRI
- As the battery approaches replacement, so the timing can be planned calmly

Stored recordings can also reveal silent [atrial fibrillation](/conditions/atrial-fibrillation), which may change how stroke risk is managed.

## How to prepare

There is very little to do, which is one of the advantages of this visit.

- Eat, drink, and take your medicines normally unless your team says otherwise.
- Bring your device identification card. It lists the manufacturer and model, which the programmer needs.
- Bring an up-to-date medication list, including anything new started by another doctor.
- Wear a two-piece outfit with a top that opens easily at the chest.
- Write down any symptoms and the dates they occurred. Matching a symptom to a stored recording is often the most useful part of the visit.
- Mention any recent falls, shocks, hospital visits, or planned imaging.
- Allow about forty five minutes at the office, including check-in.

## What happens during the procedure

You sit or lie down in an exam chair. Your shirt stays on in most cases. A programming wand is placed over the pacemaker site, or the device connects wirelessly if it supports that. You will not feel the connection.

The programmer downloads stored information. Your team reviews battery voltage and projected remaining life, lead impedance and thresholds, and the percentage of time the device has been pacing. Recorded rhythm strips are examined for pauses, fast atrial rhythms, and any [arrhythmias](/conditions/arrhythmias) that occurred between visits.

Brief testing follows. The device may be told to pace slightly faster or slower for a few seconds to confirm the leads capture the heart reliably. Some patients feel a fluttering or a mild change in heartbeat during those seconds. It is not painful and it stops immediately. Settings may then be adjusted, for example raising the lower rate limit so you feel less tired with activity. A summary is printed and stored in your chart. Sometimes an [electrocardiogram](/services/electrocardiogram-ecg-ekg) is recorded alongside the interrogation for comparison.

## Recovery and results

There is no recovery. You return to normal activity immediately, including driving and work.

Most results are available during the visit itself. Dr. Nanavati will tell you the battery status in plain terms, usually as an estimate in years, and whether the leads look stable. If the battery is nearing the end of its service, a generator change is scheduled well in advance. That is a short procedure that replaces the device while keeping the existing leads in place when they are working well.

If stored recordings show a new rhythm problem, next steps may include a medication change, further monitoring such as [Holter monitoring](/services/holter-monitoring), or an [echocardiogram](/services/echocardiogram) to check how the heart is pumping. Your next interrogation date is set before you leave.

## Risks and safety

Interrogation is one of the safest procedures in cardiology. Nothing enters the body, there is no radiation, and no sedation is needed.

The small points worth knowing are these. During brief testing you may feel a few seconds of fluttering, skipped beats, or a faster pulse. Some patients feel a moment of lightheadedness while pacing settings are tested. These sensations end as soon as the test does. Very rarely, a device needs reprogramming during the visit because a setting is no longer suitable, which is exactly what the check is designed to catch.

Between visits, keep your device card with you, avoid resting a mobile phone directly over the generator, and tell every clinician you see that you have a pacemaker. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/pacemakersandimplantabledefibrillators.html) has practical guidance on living with an implanted cardiac device.
