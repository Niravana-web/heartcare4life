## What is transthoracic echocardiography?

Transthoracic echocardiography, usually shortened to TTE, is an ultrasound scan of the heart. The pictures are taken through the chest wall. A small handheld probe called a transducer is pressed against the skin. A layer of gel sits between them. The probe sends harmless high-frequency sound waves into the chest. It then listens for the echoes that bounce back from heart muscle, valves, and blood. A computer turns those echoes into moving pictures on a screen. The study shows the size and shape of each chamber. It also shows how strongly the heart squeezes. You can see whether valves open and close properly. Blood movement through the heart is visible too. There is no radiation, no needles, and no dye. Nothing is inserted into the body. Most scans take thirty to forty five minutes. You lie on an exam table throughout. This is the standard echocardiogram. It is the most common heart imaging test performed.

The scan can be done in the office. That makes it a practical first look when heart symptoms appear.

## Why Dr. Nanavati may recommend this

An [echocardiogram](/services/echocardiogram) answers questions that a physical exam and an [electrocardiogram](/services/electrocardiogram-ecg-ekg) cannot. It shows structure and pumping function rather than electrical activity.

Common reasons include:

- Shortness of breath, fatigue, swelling in the legs, or reduced exercise tolerance.
- A [heart murmur](/conditions/heart-murmur) heard during an exam.
- [Chest pain](/conditions/chest-pain) that needs a broader heart evaluation.
- Known or suspected valve disease, and periodic follow-up once it is diagnosed.
- Assessment of the heart's pumping strength, reported as the ejection fraction.
- Follow-up after a heart attack, or during treatment that can affect the heart, such as some chemotherapy.
- High blood pressure that may have thickened the heart muscle.

The [National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/heart-tests) describes the range of problems echocardiography can identify.

## How to prepare

Preparation is minimal. That is one reason this test is used so often.

- No fasting is needed for a standard study. Eat a light meal rather than a large one. A full stomach can push against the diaphragm and blur images.
- Take your usual medicines unless you are told otherwise.
- Shower beforehand. Skip lotions, oils, powders, and body sprays on your chest. They interfere with gel contact.
- Wear a two-piece outfit. You will undress from the waist up and wear a gown that opens at the front.
- Bring a list of your medicines and any previous heart test results.
- Allow about an hour at the office, including check-in and gowning.
- Tell the technologist about recent chest surgery, breathing difficulty lying flat, or chest tenderness.

## What happens during the procedure

You lie on an exam table, usually on your back first. You are then turned onto your left side. Turning to the left brings the heart closer to the chest wall. That improves the pictures. Small electrodes are placed on your chest or limbs. They track your heartbeat during the scan.

Warm gel is applied to the chest. The technologist is called a sonographer. He or she presses the transducer against the skin. The probe moves slowly between the ribs, below the breastbone, and at the base of the neck. Firm pressure is sometimes needed to get a clear window. That can feel uncomfortable but should not be painful. Say so if it is.

You will hear whooshing and pulsing sounds. Those are Doppler recordings of blood flowing. They are a normal part of the test. You may be asked to hold your breath briefly or to breathe out fully. You may also be asked to stay still while a clip is recorded. The room is often dim. This helps the sonographer see the screen clearly.

## Recovery and results

There is no recovery period. The gel is wiped off, you dress, and you leave. Driving, eating, working, and exercise are all fine immediately.

The sonographer records the images. A cardiologist interprets them. Measurements are compared against normal ranges. The moving clips are reviewed frame by frame. A written report is usually available within one to a few business days.

Dr. Nanavati will explain the findings in practical terms. He will say how strongly the heart is pumping. He will also say whether any valve needs watching. Many findings simply mean a repeat scan in a year. Others lead to further testing. [Advanced stress testing](/services/advanced-stress-testing) follows if reduced blood flow is suspected. A [transesophageal echocardiogram](/services/transesophageal-echocardiography) gives a closer view of a valve or the upper chambers. Treatment may involve medication changes, blood pressure control, or referral for valve care.

## Risks and safety

Transthoracic echocardiography is among the safest tests in medicine. Ultrasound at diagnostic levels has no known harmful effects. The study can be repeated as often as needed. It is safe in pregnancy and safe for children.

The only discomforts are minor. Pressure from the transducer can be tender, especially over the ribs or after chest surgery. The gel feels cool at first, though it is often warmed. Some patients find lying on the left side awkward. Electrode stickers occasionally irritate sensitive skin.

One honest limitation is worth knowing. Image quality depends on your body's acoustic windows. Lung disease, obesity, chest deformity, or scarring can make pictures harder to obtain. A contrast agent may then be used to brighten the chamber borders. A different test may be recommended instead. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/heartdiseases.html) offers a general overview of heart conditions this study helps evaluate.
