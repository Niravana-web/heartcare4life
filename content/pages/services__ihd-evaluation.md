## What is an IHD Evaluation?

An IHD evaluation is the organized workup used to find out whether your heart muscle is getting enough blood. IHD stands for ischemic heart disease, which means the coronary arteries that feed the heart have narrowed, usually from plaque, the fatty and calcified deposit that builds up inside artery walls. When a narrowed artery cannot deliver enough blood during exertion, the heart muscle becomes starved of oxygen. That is ischemia, and it often shows up as chest pressure, breathlessness, or unusual fatigue. The evaluation is a sequence rather than a single test. It starts with your history and examination, adds blood work and an electrocardiogram, and then uses imaging or stress testing to answer two questions: is blood flow actually reduced, and if so, how much heart muscle is affected. The answers determine whether medication, lifestyle change, or a procedure is the right treatment. Dr. Vimal Nanavati coordinates the whole sequence.

## Why Dr. Nanavati may recommend this

Ischemic heart disease is the most common form of heart disease, and it is often treatable long before a heart attack occurs. The [National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute describes coronary heart disease and its causes](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/coronary-heart-disease), and the [American Heart Association explains the warning signs of a heart attack](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/heart-attack).

Dr. Nanavati may recommend an evaluation if you have:

- [Chest pain](/conditions/chest-pain), pressure, tightness, or burning, especially when it comes on with exertion and eases with rest
- Discomfort spreading to the jaw, neck, shoulder, arm, or upper back
- Shortness of breath with activity, or a clear drop in stamina
- Nausea, sweating, or unusual fatigue with exertion, which are common presentations in women and in people with diabetes
- Several risk factors together, such as high blood pressure, high cholesterol, diabetes, smoking, or a strong family history
- An abnormal electrocardiogram or an incidental finding of coronary calcium on another scan
- A previous heart attack, stent, or bypass, where recurring symptoms need reassessment

Sudden severe chest pain, especially with sweating, nausea, or breathlessness, is an emergency. Call 911 rather than booking an appointment.

## How to prepare

Because the evaluation involves several tests, preparation depends on which ones are scheduled.

- Bring every medication and supplement you take, with doses, plus records of previous cardiac testing.
- Write down your symptom pattern before the visit. What brings it on, how long it lasts, what relieves it, and whether it is changing.
- Note your family history, particularly heart attack or sudden death in a parent or sibling before age 60.
- For fasting blood work, expect 9 to 12 hours of water only if you are told to fast.
- For stress testing, avoid caffeine for 12 to 24 hours, wear walking shoes and comfortable clothing, and ask which medications to hold that morning.
- For CT imaging, expect a period without food, and report any allergy to contrast dye and any kidney problems.
- Ask whether you will need a driver, which is usually only the case if sedation is planned.
- Allow extra time. Several appointments across a few weeks is normal.

## What happens during the procedure

An IHD evaluation is a series of steps rather than one procedure, and not everyone needs all of them.

It begins with a consultation. Dr. Nanavati takes a detailed history, examines you, checks blood pressure in both arms, and listens to the heart, lungs, and major arteries.

Blood tests follow. These typically include cholesterol and triglycerides, blood sugar or hemoglobin A1c, kidney and liver function, thyroid function, and a blood count. Advanced lipid testing may be added when risk is unclear.

An [electrocardiogram](/services/electrocardiogram-ecg-ekg) records the heart's electrical activity and can show evidence of a prior heart attack or ongoing strain. An [echocardiogram](/services/echocardiogram) shows chamber size, valve function, and pumping strength, and can reveal areas of muscle that move poorly because of reduced blood supply.

Functional testing comes next when needed. A treadmill or medication-based stress test, sometimes with imaging such as [advanced stress testing](/services/advanced-stress-testing), shows whether blood flow falls short under demand.

Anatomic imaging such as [coronary CT angiography](/services/coronary-ct-angiography) pictures the arteries directly. If the findings suggest a severe blockage, a coronary angiogram in a hospital catheterization laboratory maps the arteries precisely and allows treatment during the same procedure.

## Recovery and results

Most of the evaluation involves no recovery at all. You can drive, work, and exercise immediately after blood tests, an electrocardiogram, an echocardiogram, or a stress test, unless you were sedated or told otherwise.

Results arrive at different speeds. An electrocardiogram is read the same day. Routine blood work usually returns in a day or two, while advanced panels take longer. Imaging reports generally reach Dr. Nanavati within a few days. He then puts the pieces together and explains the overall picture rather than sending isolated numbers.

The outcome falls into three broad paths. If testing is normal, the focus shifts to prevention and to finding another explanation for your symptoms. If mild to moderate disease is found, treatment centers on medication, cholesterol and blood pressure control, diabetes management, smoking cessation, and structured exercise, which together are highly effective. If a severe blockage is found, the discussion turns to a procedure such as [angioplasty](/services/angioplasty) or bypass surgery, followed by cardiac rehabilitation.

## Risks and safety

The evaluation as a whole is low risk, and each step carries its own small and specific risks.

Blood draws cause a brief sting and occasional bruising. Electrocardiography and echocardiography carry no risk at all, since both are passive and use no radiation.

Stress testing carries a small risk, because it deliberately makes the heart work harder. Chest discomfort, breathlessness, and fatigue are expected. Serious events such as a heart attack or a dangerous rhythm are rare, and testing is supervised with monitoring and emergency equipment on hand.

Tests using contrast dye can cause allergic reactions, usually mild, and can temporarily affect kidney function, particularly in people with kidney disease or diabetes. CT imaging and nuclear scans involve radiation exposure, which is kept as low as possible and justified by the information gained.

Coronary angiography is the most invasive step and carries small risks of bleeding at the access site, artery injury, and rarely heart attack or stroke.

The guiding principle is that each test should be ordered only when its result will change what happens next.
