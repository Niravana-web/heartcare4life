## What is percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty?

Percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty, or PTCA, is a catheter procedure. It reopens a narrowed artery supplying the heart muscle. A thin flexible tube is passed into an artery at the wrist or groin. X-ray pictures guide it to the heart. A very small balloon at the tip is positioned inside the narrowing. The balloon is then inflated for a few seconds. It presses the fatty plaque outward and widens the channel. Blood can flow again. In most cases a stent is placed in the same spot to hold the artery open. A stent is a tiny mesh tube. No chest incision is made. You stay awake with numbing medicine and light sedation. PTCA is often called balloon angioplasty. Together with stenting it forms percutaneous coronary intervention, or PCI. It relieves angina. During a heart attack, it restores flow quickly to limit damage.

Dr. Nanavati performs these interventional procedures at affiliated hospitals rather than in the office.

## Why Dr. Nanavati may recommend this

PTCA treats [coronary artery disease](/conditions/coronary-artery-disease). Plaque narrows the heart arteries in this condition. The [National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/coronary-heart-disease) explains how this narrowing reduces oxygen delivery to heart muscle.

It may be recommended when:

- [Chest pain](/conditions/chest-pain) or breathlessness continues despite medication.
- Testing shows a significant blockage that is limiting blood flow.
- You are having a heart attack, where opening the artery quickly is the priority.
- Angina is limiting the activities you want to do.
- A previously treated artery has narrowed again.

Not every blockage needs a balloon. Stable symptoms are often treated first with medicines and risk factor control. Findings from a [nuclear stress test](/services/nuclear-stress-test) or coronary CT angiography also help. They show whether an intervention will genuinely help. Some patients are better served by bypass surgery. That is especially true when several arteries are involved.

## How to prepare

- Expect to fast from midnight, or for at least six to eight hours, before a planned procedure.
- Ask which medicines to hold and which to take with a sip of water. Blood thinners, diabetes drugs, and diuretics all need specific instructions.
- Report allergies, especially to contrast dye, shellfish, or latex. Mention any prior reaction to imaging dye.
- Tell your team about kidney problems. Contrast dye is filtered by the kidneys, so extra fluids may be given.
- Bring a complete list of medicines and doses, including supplements.
- Arrange a driver and someone to stay with you the first night at home.
- Bring loose clothing. Plan for an overnight stay in case one is needed.

Blood tests, an electrocardiogram, and sometimes a chest X-ray are done beforehand.

## What happens during the procedure

You lie on a padded table in the cardiac catheterization laboratory. Monitoring pads and a blood pressure cuff go on. An intravenous line delivers mild sedation. You stay awake but relaxed. Many patients remember little of it.

Next the access site is numbed. That is either the wrist for a [transradial angiogram](/services/transradial-angiogram) or the groin for a [transfemoral angiogram](/services/transfemoral-angiogram). A small sheath is placed in the artery. Catheters pass through the sheath without pain. Arteries have no sensory nerves inside. Contrast dye is injected next. It makes the arteries show up on the X-ray screen. The dye may bring a brief warm flush.

The narrowing is then identified. A fine guidewire is passed across it. The balloon is advanced over the wire. Inflation lasts only seconds. Flow is briefly interrupted, so you may feel pressure or short chest discomfort. Tell the team. This is expected and manageable. A stent is usually deployed next. Final pictures confirm improved flow. Most procedures take thirty to ninety minutes.

## Recovery and results

The sheath is removed. The site is sealed with pressure, a band, or a closure device. Wrist access allows sitting up and walking sooner. Groin access requires lying flat for several hours. That prevents bleeding.

Many stable patients go home the next day. After a heart attack the stay is longer. Bruising at the access site is common. It fades over one to two weeks. Avoid heavy lifting and strenuous activity for about a week. Follow your specific instructions on driving and returning to work.

Dr. Nanavati can usually describe the result immediately after the procedure. He often shows you the images. Antiplatelet medication is essential after a stent. That is typically aspirin plus a second drug. Do not stop these without speaking to your cardiologist. Stopping early raises the risk of a clot forming in the stent. [Cardiac rehabilitation](/services/cardiac-rehabilitation) is strongly encouraged. It improves both fitness and long-term outcomes.

## Risks and safety

PTCA is a well established procedure with a strong safety record. It is still a heart procedure. Possible complications include:

- Bleeding, bruising, or a lump at the access site.
- Allergic reaction to contrast dye.
- Kidney strain from the dye, more likely with existing kidney disease.
- Clot formation or re-narrowing inside the treated artery over time.
- Damage to the artery wall.
- Irregular heart rhythms.
- Rarely, heart attack, stroke, or the need for emergency bypass surgery.

Risk depends on several things. Your age, your kidney function, and how many arteries are involved all matter. So does whether the procedure is urgent. The [American Heart Association](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/heart-attack) describes why rapid treatment matters most during a heart attack.
