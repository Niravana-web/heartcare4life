## The short answer

Both procedures address the same problem: a leg vein whose one way valves have failed, so blood pools instead of returning to the heart. Vein stripping is the older surgical answer. Through incisions at the groin and lower leg, the surgeon physically pulls the faulty vein out. Venous ablation is the catheter based answer. A thin fiber or catheter is placed inside the vein under ultrasound guidance and the vein is sealed shut from within, using heat, a medical adhesive, or a chemical agent. Your body then reroutes blood through healthy deeper veins. For most people with reflux in the great or small saphenous vein, ablation has become the preferred first approach, because outcomes are comparable while pain, bruising, and time away from work are substantially lower. Stripping remains useful for particular anatomy. And for many patients, compression and conservative care should come first.

## How they differ at a glance

| | Venous ablation | Vein stripping |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it involves | A catheter or fiber is placed inside the vein through a needle puncture. Heat, adhesive, or a sclerosing agent seals the vein closed. | The vein is tied off and physically removed through incisions at the groin and lower leg. |
| Where it is done | In the office procedure room, guided by ultrasound. No hospital stay. | A hospital or surgical center operating room, performed by a vascular or general surgeon. |
| Anesthesia or sedation | Local anesthetic, usually with dilute tumescent anesthesia along the vein. You stay awake. | General or spinal anesthesia in most cases. |
| Typical recovery | Walking immediately. Compression stockings for a period afterward. Most people return to desk work within a day or two. | One to three weeks before normal activity. More bruising, more soreness, and incisions that need wound care. |
| Best suited for | Saphenous vein reflux with suitable vein size and a reasonably straight course. | Very large, tortuous, or aneurysmal veins, veins lying very close to the skin, and some recurrent cases after prior treatment. |

## Venous ablation in detail

[Venous ablation](/services/venous-ablation) works by closing the diseased vein rather than removing it. Once sealed, the vein gradually shrinks into a fibrous cord and is absorbed. Blood that used to pool there returns through the deep venous system, which carries the great majority of leg blood flow anyway.

Thermal methods use radiofrequency energy or laser light. They require tumescent anesthesia, a dilute anesthetic solution injected around the vein that numbs it and protects nearby nerves and skin. Non thermal methods use a medical adhesive or a chemical agent and need less injected anesthetic.

The advantages are consistent across studies: less postoperative pain, less bruising, faster return to work, and fewer wound complications, with closure rates and symptom relief comparable to surgery. The [National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/varicose-veins) describes these minimally invasive options alongside traditional surgery.

Ablation has limits. It treats the underlying refluxing trunk, but visible surface varicosities often need additional treatment such as sclerotherapy or small phlebectomy. Possible complications include skin discoloration along the treated vein, temporary nerve irritation causing numbness, and rarely a clot extending toward the deep veins, which is why a follow up ultrasound is standard.

## Vein stripping in detail

Vein stripping and high ligation dominated varicose vein treatment for most of the twentieth century, and it works. The refluxing vein is disconnected where it joins the deep system and then removed along its length.

Because the vein is physically gone, it cannot recanalize, which is to say it cannot partially reopen the way a sealed vein occasionally does. Surgeons still choose stripping for veins that are unusually wide, badly twisted, aneurysmal, or so superficial that heating them would risk burning the skin. It is also considered in some patients with recurrent varicose veins after previous procedures.

The costs are real. General or spinal anesthesia carries its own risks. Recovery involves more pain and bruising, incisions require care, and return to work takes longer. Wound infection and saphenous nerve injury are recognized complications. Patient oriented explanations of both treatment routes are available from [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/varicoseveins.html).

## How Dr. Nanavati decides which is right for you

Everything starts with a map. A [venous ultrasound](/services/venous-ultrasound) shows which veins are refluxing, how large they are, how deep they run, and whether any clot is present. Treating varicose veins without that study is guesswork.

Conservative care comes first for most patients. Graduated compression stockings, leg elevation, weight management, and walking often control aching, heaviness, and swelling. A trial of this is reasonable before any procedure, and for some people it is the whole treatment.

Symptoms have to justify intervention. Aching, throbbing, night cramps, swelling, skin thickening, and healed or open ulcers near the ankle are medical findings. Appearance alone is a different conversation.

Anatomy then narrows the options. Vein diameter, tortuosity, depth beneath the skin, and proximity to nerves all influence whether ablation will work well and safely.

Other causes must be excluded. Leg swelling can come from heart failure, kidney disease, or lymphatic problems rather than veins. When cardiac causes are suspected, an office [echocardiogram](/services/echocardiogram) helps sort it out.

Clot history changes the plan. A previous deep vein thrombosis alters how the deep system functions, and treatment must account for that. Background on venous clotting is available from the [American Heart Association](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/venous-thromboembolism) and [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/deepveinthrombosis.html).

## Questions to ask your cardiologist

- Does my ultrasound actually show reflux, and in which specific veins?
- Are my symptoms medical, or is my main concern the appearance of the veins?
- Have I given compression therapy a fair trial first?
- Is my vein a good candidate for ablation, or does its size or course argue for surgery?
- Will I need additional treatment for the surface veins after the main vein is treated?
- What follow up imaging is done afterward, and what would it be checking for?
