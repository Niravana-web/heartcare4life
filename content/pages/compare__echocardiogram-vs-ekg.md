## The short answer

These two tests answer different questions, so one does not replace the other. An EKG, also written ECG or electrocardiogram, records the heart's electrical signals through stickers placed on your chest, arms, and legs. It takes about five minutes and shows rhythm problems, evidence of a past heart attack, and electrical strain patterns. An echocardiogram is an ultrasound picture of the heart in motion. It shows chamber size, valve function, the thickness of the muscle walls, fluid around the heart, and how strongly the heart squeezes. If your cardiologist wants to know how your heart is behaving electrically, the EKG answers that. If the question is how your heart is built and how well it pumps, the echocardiogram answers that. Most cardiology evaluations begin with an EKG, because it is quick and inexpensive, then add an echocardiogram when structure or pump function is in question.

## How they differ at a glance

| | Electrocardiogram (EKG or ECG) | Echocardiogram |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it involves | Ten adhesive electrodes record the heart's electrical activity as a tracing on paper or screen. Nothing is injected. | An ultrasound probe with gel is moved across your chest. Sound waves create moving images of the heart and its valves. |
| Where it is done | In the office, at the bedside, or in an emergency room. Performed in office at HeartCare4life. | In the office ultrasound room. Performed in office at HeartCare4life. |
| Anesthesia or sedation | None. You lie still and breathe normally. | None for a standard chest wall study. Sedation applies only to the swallowed probe version, done in hospital. |
| Typical recovery | None. You leave immediately and drive yourself home. | None. Gel is wiped off and you resume normal activity. |
| Best suited for | Rhythm disturbances, suspected heart attack, conduction blocks, and rapid screening for chest pain. | Heart murmurs, valve disease, heart failure, shortness of breath, and measuring ejection fraction. |

## Electrocardiogram in detail

An [electrocardiogram](/services/electrocardiogram-ecg-ekg) captures the electrical wave that travels through the heart with every beat. Electrodes on the skin sense that wave from twelve angles, which is why it is often called a twelve lead EKG.

Its strength is speed and breadth. Within seconds a cardiologist can see whether the rhythm is regular, whether the upper and lower chambers are communicating normally, whether the pattern suggests an artery is blocked right now, and whether an old heart attack left a scar. In [chest pain](/conditions/chest-pain) evaluation it is the first test performed, because it separates emergencies from everything else. The [American Heart Association](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/heart-attack/diagnosing-a-heart-attack/electrocardiogram-ecg-or-ekg) describes this role in detail.

Its limitation is the snapshot. An EKG records perhaps ten seconds. If your palpitations arrive twice a week, the tracing will very likely be normal. That is when a wearable monitor is needed instead, a topic covered on our [Holter vs event monitor](/compare/holter-vs-event-monitor) page and on the [arrhythmias](/conditions/arrhythmias) page.

An EKG also says little about valves. A leaking valve can produce a perfectly normal tracing.

## Echocardiogram in detail

An [echocardiogram](/services/echocardiogram) uses sound waves rather than radiation. The standard version performed in our offices is [transthoracic echocardiography](/services/transthoracic-echocardiography), meaning the probe rests on the outside of the chest.

It produces a live moving image. Doppler settings add color and sound to show the direction and speed of blood flow, which is how valve leaks and narrowings are graded. The single most quoted result is the ejection fraction, the percentage of blood the left ventricle pushes out with each beat. A normal value sits roughly between 55 and 70 percent. The [American Heart Association echocardiogram page](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/heart-attack/diagnosing-a-heart-attack/echocardiogram-echo) explains what the numbers mean.

Echocardiography also finds pericardial fluid, thickened muscle from long standing high blood pressure, congenital abnormalities, and clots inside the chambers.

Its limitation is that it does not read rhythm. It also depends on image quality, which can be poor in patients with lung disease or a larger body habitus. In those cases another imaging test may be needed.

## How Dr. Nanavati decides which is right for you

The symptom drives the test. Palpitations, fainting, or a pulse that feels irregular point toward the electrical side, so the EKG comes first. Shortness of breath, ankle swelling, a murmur heard on exam, or known valve disease point toward ultrasound.

Sequence matters more than choice. Almost every new cardiology patient gets an EKG, because it is fast and it changes decisions. The echocardiogram is added when the history, the exam, or the EKG itself raises a structural question.

Abnormal EKG findings often trigger an echocardiogram. Voltage suggesting thickened walls, a pattern of prior infarction, or a new bundle branch block all justify imaging the muscle directly.

Neither test measures blood flow under stress. If exertional chest pain is the problem, a stress study is the right next step. Our [treadmill vs nuclear stress test](/compare/treadmill-vs-nuclear-stress-test) page explains those options.

Both tests are safe, painless, and free of radiation, so cost and yield rather than risk usually decide the order. General orientation on cardiac testing is available from [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/hearthealthtests.html) and the [National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/heart-tests).

## Questions to ask your cardiologist

- Which specific question are we trying to answer with this test?
- Will an EKG alone be enough, or should we image the heart structure too?
- What is my ejection fraction, and is it in the normal range?
- If my symptoms come and go, would a longer rhythm monitor be more useful?
- Do my results need to be repeated, and if so, how often?
- Does anything on these results change my medications?
