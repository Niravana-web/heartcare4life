## What is a transfemoral angiogram?

A transfemoral angiogram is an X-ray study of your arteries. It is performed through the femoral artery in the groin. The skin is numbed first. A thin tube called a catheter is then placed into this large artery. The entry point sits near the crease of the leg. The catheter is guided through the body to the heart, the legs, or whichever vessels need study. Contrast dye is injected through it. An X-ray camera records the flow. Narrowing, blockage, aneurysm, and abnormal connections all become visible on these images. You stay awake with light sedation. The study itself usually takes under an hour. The femoral artery is large. It accommodates bigger catheters and more complex equipment than the wrist can. That makes this route the choice for many advanced procedures. Treatment can often follow through the same access during the same visit.

Doctors also call this femoral access. It is diagnostic imaging. It guides what treatment follows.

## Why Dr. Nanavati may recommend this

An angiogram is ordered when symptoms and non-invasive testing point toward significant arterial disease. The details are still unclear at that stage.

Reasons include:

- Ongoing [chest pain](/conditions/chest-pain) or breathlessness that suggests reduced blood flow to the heart.
- Known or suspected [coronary artery disease](/conditions/coronary-artery-disease) that may need treatment.
- Leg pain with walking, non-healing wounds, or other signs of peripheral artery disease.
- An abnormal stress test or vascular study.
- Evaluation of an [aortic aneurysm](/conditions/aortic-aneurysm) or other vessel abnormality.
- Planning for a structural heart or vascular procedure that needs larger equipment.

The [National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/cardiac-catheterization) describes how catheter-based artery studies are performed and used.

### Groin versus wrist access

The alternative is a [transradial angiogram](/services/transradial-angiogram) through the wrist. Image quality is the same either way. The tradeoffs are practical.

The groin route uses a much larger artery. It accepts larger sheaths and bulkier devices. It also gives a straighter path to certain targets. Those include the leg arteries and the aorta. Complex and structural procedures often require it. It is the fallback when arm vessels are too small, too twisted, or already used.

Its drawback is recovery. The femoral artery is deep, so bleeding is harder to control. You must lie flat with the leg straight for several hours afterward. Bleeding and bruising complications are more common than with wrist access. A groin bleed can also be more serious. It can track into the abdomen unseen. Many straightforward heart studies are therefore done through the wrist first. Dr. Nanavati weighs your anatomy, body weight, bleeding risk, kidney function, and the equipment required before choosing.

## How to prepare

- Fast from midnight, or for at least six to eight hours, unless instructed otherwise.
- Confirm which medicines to hold. Blood thinners, metformin, and diuretics each need specific direction.
- Report allergies to contrast dye, iodine, shellfish, or latex. Mention any past reaction to imaging dye.
- Tell the team about kidney disease. Dye is filtered by the kidneys. Extra fluids may be given.
- Mention prior groin surgery, hernia repair, or vascular grafts in the leg.
- Do not shave the groin yourself. Staff will clip hair if needed.
- Arrange a driver and someone to stay with you the first night.
- Allow most of the day. Lying flat afterward takes several hours.

## What happens during the procedure

You lie flat on an X-ray table. Monitoring pads, a blood pressure cuff, and an oxygen sensor go on. An intravenous line delivers mild sedation. The groin is cleaned and draped. Local anesthetic then numbs it. You feel a sting, then steady pressure.

A short sheath is placed into the femoral artery. Ultrasound guidance often helps find the vessel precisely. Catheters pass through the sheath and travel to the target vessels. This part is painless. The inside of an artery has no pain nerves. Contrast dye is then injected. A warm flush through the body is normal and brief. The camera moves around you and takes pictures from several angles. You may be asked to hold your breath for a moment.

A significant blockage may be found. Treatment such as [angioplasty](/services/angioplasty) or coronary stenting can often be done immediately. That avoids a second procedure.

## Recovery and results

At the end the sheath is removed. Firm pressure is then held over the site. A closure device may be used to seal the artery instead. You then lie flat with that leg straight. This typically lasts two to six hours, depending on the method used. Nurses check the site and the pulses in your foot regularly.

Walking begins gradually once you are cleared to move. At home, avoid lifting, straining, and vigorous activity for about a week. Avoid stairs where possible. Keep the site clean and dry. Bruising is common and can look dramatic. It fades over one to two weeks.

Call immediately for sudden swelling, a growing lump, or fresh bleeding. Severe groin or back pain also needs a call. So does a cold, pale, or numb leg. Dr. Nanavati usually reviews the images with you the same day. The plan depends on the findings. It may involve medication, stenting, surgery, or [cardiac rehabilitation](/services/cardiac-rehabilitation).

## Risks and safety

Diagnostic angiography is low risk for most patients. It is still an invasive test. Possible complications include:

- Bruising, bleeding, or a firm lump at the groin.
- Pseudoaneurysm, a contained bulge where the artery was entered.
- Arteriovenous fistula, an abnormal connection between the artery and a nearby vein.
- Allergic reaction to contrast dye.
- Kidney strain from dye, especially with existing kidney disease.
- Blood clot or injury to the artery.
- Rarely, heart attack, stroke, or serious bleeding.

The [American Heart Association](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/peripheral-artery-disease) provides background on artery disease outside the heart. This study often evaluates that disease.
