## What is ventricular tachycardia?

Ventricular tachycardia, often shortened to VT, is a fast heart rhythm that starts in the ventricles, the heart's two lower pumping chambers. It is defined as three or more consecutive beats from the ventricles at a rate above 100 beats per minute. Because the signal bypasses the heart's normal wiring, the chambers do not fill properly between beats, so less blood reaches the body with each contraction. Short runs lasting a few seconds are called non sustained VT and may cause only a flutter. Runs lasting longer than 30 seconds, or any run that drops your blood pressure, are called sustained VT and are a medical emergency. VT most often occurs in a heart scarred by a previous heart attack, weakened by cardiomyopathy, or affected by an inherited electrical disorder. Untreated sustained VT can deteriorate into ventricular fibrillation and cardiac arrest, which is why the [American Heart Association](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/arrhythmia) classifies it among the most serious arrhythmias.

## Symptoms to watch for

VT often announces itself abruptly. How it feels depends on how fast it runs and how strong your heart muscle is.

Call the office if you have:

- Brief flutters or a run of rapid beats that stops on its own
- Palpitations with mild dizziness that resolve quickly
- Reduced exercise tolerance or new fatigue
- Known heart disease and any new rhythm sensation
- Shocks or beeping from an implanted defibrillator, which need prompt review

Call 911 immediately for:

- A sustained racing heartbeat that does not slow down
- Fainting or near fainting
- Chest pain or pressure with a racing heart
- Severe shortness of breath, confusion or clammy, gray skin
- More than one defibrillator shock in 24 hours

If someone collapses and is not breathing normally, call 911, start CPR and use an AED. Survival from [cardiac arrest](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/cardiac-arrest) depends on how quickly CPR and defibrillation begin.

## How Dr. Nanavati diagnoses it

VT must be documented on a tracing, and the heart muscle underneath it must be assessed. Both parts drive treatment.

- **[Electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG)](/services/electrocardiogram-ecg-ekg)** captures the rhythm and shows evidence of a prior heart attack that could be the source.
- **[Holter monitoring](/services/holter-monitoring)** records every beat for 24 to 48 hours to find non sustained runs.
- **[7 to 10 day ambulatory rhythm monitoring](/services/7-10-day-ambulatory-rhythm-monitor)** extends the search for episodes that are less frequent.
- **[Loop recorder implantation and interrogation](/services/loop-recorder-implantation-and-interrogation)** monitors long term after unexplained fainting.
- **[Echocardiogram](/services/echocardiogram)** measures the ejection fraction, the single most important number for judging risk.
- **[Nuclear stress testing](/services/nuclear-stress-test)** and **[advanced stress testing](/services/advanced-stress-testing)** look for reduced blood flow that provokes the rhythm.
- **[Ischemic heart disease evaluation](/services/ihd-evaluation)** assesses [coronary artery disease](/conditions/coronary-artery-disease) as the underlying cause.
- **[Defibrillator interrogation](/services/defibrillator-interrogation)** and **[pacemaker interrogation](/services/pacemaker-interrogation)** read stored episodes and check device settings.

If a blockage is suspected, a [transradial angiogram](/services/transradial-angiogram) at an affiliated hospital defines the coronary anatomy. Electrolyte, magnesium and thyroid testing plus [advanced lipid testing](/services/advanced-lipid-testing) complete the picture.

## Treatment options

Treatment has two aims: stop episodes, and prevent sudden cardiac arrest. Nearly every patient needs both addressed.

**Reverse the triggers.** Low potassium and magnesium, stimulants, alcohol, certain decongestants and some prescription drugs that prolong the QT interval can all provoke VT. Reviewing every medication and supplement is part of the first visit.

**Treat the underlying heart.** Restoring blood flow with [angioplasty](/services/angioplasty) and [coronary stenting](/services/coronary-stenting) removes the trigger when ischemia is the cause. Medications that strengthen and protect a weakened heart muscle reduce episodes over time, following [American College of Cardiology guidelines](https://www.acc.org/Guidelines).

**Medication.** Beta blockers are the foundation. Antiarrhythmic drugs such as amiodarone or sotalol are added for recurrent episodes and are monitored closely.

**Devices and ablation.** An implantable cardioverter defibrillator monitors continuously and delivers a shock to stop a lethal rhythm. It is the standard protection for patients with a significantly weakened pumping function. Catheter ablation targets the scar tissue circuit driving VT and can markedly reduce episodes and shocks. In urgent hospital settings a [temporary pacemaker](/services/temporary-pacemaker) may be used. [Cardiac rehabilitation](/services/cardiac-rehabilitation) follows, so you can return to activity under supervision.

## What to expect at your visit

Bring your medication list, any hospital discharge paperwork, prior ECGs, and your device card if you have a pacemaker or defibrillator. Describe every episode: how it started, how long it lasted, whether you fainted and what stopped it. The visit includes an exam, blood pressure and an ECG, with device interrogation and echocardiography performed in the office. You will leave with a specific plan covering medication, monitoring, activity limits and exactly when to call 911. Family members are welcome, and CPR training is encouraged. The [FAQs page](/faqs) answers common questions.

## When to see a cardiologist

Seek prompt evaluation after any fainting episode, a documented run of VT, a defibrillator shock, or palpitations with [chest pain](/conditions/chest-pain) or breathlessness. Anyone with a prior heart attack, a weakened heart muscle, or a family history of sudden death before age 50 should be assessed.
