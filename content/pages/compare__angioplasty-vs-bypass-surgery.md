## The short answer

Angioplasty and bypass surgery both restore blood flow to heart muscle starved by blocked arteries. Angioplasty is a catheter procedure. A cardiologist threads a thin tube through an artery in your wrist or groin, opens the narrowing with a balloon, and usually leaves a small metal mesh stent behind. Bypass surgery is open heart surgery. A cardiac surgeon builds new routes around the blockages using vessels borrowed from your chest, arm, or leg. For one blocked artery, or two in a straightforward pattern, angioplasty is usually the reasonable first choice. For left main disease, for three vessel disease, and especially for people with diabetes, bypass surgery has the stronger long term survival record. The right answer depends on which arteries are narrowed, how well your heart pumps, your other medical conditions, and what matters most to you. Neither option cures the underlying disease. Both work best alongside medication, and lifestyle change.

## Angioplasty in detail

[Angioplasty](/services/angioplasty), also called percutaneous coronary intervention, reopens a narrowed artery from the inside. The technical term for the balloon step is [PTCA](/services/ptca), or percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty. In nearly all modern cases a drug eluting stent is placed during the same session. That is a tiny metal scaffold coated with medicine that discourages scar tissue from re growing. You can read more about the device itself on our [coronary stenting](/services/coronary-stenting) page.

The appeal is speed and gentleness. There is no chest incision, no heart lung machine, and no divided breastbone. In a heart attack caused by a suddenly blocked artery, angioplasty is the treatment of choice, because minutes of delay translate into permanently lost heart muscle. The [National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/percutaneous-coronary-intervention) describes it as the standard emergency approach in that setting.

The tradeoff is durability at the treated spot. Stented segments can re narrow, and you will need dual antiplatelet medication, usually aspirin plus a second drug, for a defined period afterward. Skipping those pills early raises the risk of a clot forming inside the stent.

## Bypass surgery in detail

Coronary artery bypass grafting routes blood around blockages rather than through them. Surgeons prefer the internal mammary artery from inside the chest wall for the main vessel, because arterial grafts stay open for decades. Vein grafts from the leg are used for additional targets. The [NHLBI overview of bypass grafting](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/coronary-artery-bypass-grafting) explains the operation and its risks in plain language.

Because a graft attaches beyond the diseased segment, bypass protects a whole downstream territory, not just one narrowed point. That is why it holds up better when disease is diffuse or heavily calcified. Randomized trial evidence summarized in [American College of Cardiology guidelines](https://www.acc.org/Guidelines) shows a survival advantage for surgery in patients with diabetes and multi vessel disease, and in most left main disease with complex anatomy. That is a real finding, and it points away from the catheter based option. Dr. Nanavati will tell you so directly when your anatomy fits that picture.

The cost is a harder recovery. Expect several days in hospital, weeks of restricted lifting, and a period of fatigue and disrupted sleep. Cognitive fogginess in the first weeks is common and usually temporary.

## How Dr. Nanavati decides which is right for you

The decision starts with a picture of your arteries, from a [coronary CT angiogram](/services/coronary-ct-angiography) or a catheter based angiogram. From there, several factors carry weight.

Blockage pattern matters most. Left main narrowing and disease in all three major vessels tilt strongly toward surgery. Isolated lesions in one or two vessels usually favor stenting.

Pumping function is the second factor. When an [echocardiogram](/services/echocardiogram) shows a weakened ejection fraction, surgery has generally shown better long term outcomes in trial data.

Diabetes changes the math. Coronary disease in diabetes tends to be diffuse and to progress faster, which is why guidelines favor bypass for multi vessel disease in this group.

Lesion character counts too. Heavy calcium, long segments, total occlusions, and vessels that branch at the blockage make a durable stent result harder to achieve.

Finally, competing risks matter. Advanced age, frailty, prior chest surgery, severe lung disease, or poor kidney function can make an operation riskier than the disease. In [coronary artery disease](/conditions/coronary-artery-disease) the goal is always the option that gives you the most good years, not the most aggressive intervention.

For stable symptoms without high risk anatomy, medication first is a legitimate third path. That comparison is covered on our [stent vs medication](/compare/stent-vs-medication) page.

Complex cases deserve a heart team review, a cardiologist and a cardiac surgeon reading your films together. Ask for one.

## Questions to ask your cardiologist

- Which arteries are blocked, how severely, and where exactly are the narrowings?
- Is my anatomy considered simple or complex, and does a surgeon agree with that read?
- Given my diabetes status and my ejection fraction, what does the trial evidence favor?
- If we stent, how long will I need dual antiplatelet therapy, and can I take it safely?
- What would happen if we treated this with medication and lifestyle change first?
- Has a cardiac surgeon reviewed my images, and can we hold a heart team discussion?
