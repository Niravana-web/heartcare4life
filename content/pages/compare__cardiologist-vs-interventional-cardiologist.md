## The short answer

Every interventional cardiologist is a cardiologist. The reverse is not true. A general or clinical cardiologist completes internal medicine training and then a cardiology fellowship, and spends their career diagnosing and managing heart disease with office visits, testing, and medication. An interventional cardiologist completes all of that, then adds a further year or more of dedicated training in catheter based treatment, and holds a separate board certification in interventional cardiology. That extra training covers procedures such as opening blocked coronary arteries with balloons and stents. Neither role is superior. They answer different needs. Most heart problems, including high blood pressure, cholesterol, heart failure, and stable chest pain, are managed entirely by a general cardiologist. You need an interventional cardiologist when a blockage may require catheter treatment. Many cardiologists, including Dr. Nanavati, are certified in both, so one physician can carry the case from diagnosis through treatment.

## How they differ at a glance

| | General cardiologist | Interventional cardiologist |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it involves | Internal medicine residency plus a cardiology fellowship. Diagnosis, medication management, prevention, and test interpretation. | The same training plus an additional interventional fellowship and a second board certification in catheter based treatment. |
| Where it is done | Office consultations, hospital rounds, and non invasive testing suites. | The same settings, plus the hospital cardiac catheterization lab where procedures are performed. |
| Anesthesia or sedation | None. Consultations, echocardiograms, stress tests, and monitors are all awake and painless. | None for office care. Procedures use local anesthetic at the access site with light conscious sedation. |
| Typical recovery | None. Follow up is scheduled around medication changes and repeat testing. | None after office visits. After a catheter procedure, hours of observation and several days of activity restriction. |
| Best suited for | Risk factor management, cholesterol, blood pressure, heart failure, palpitations, murmurs, and stable symptoms. | Suspected or confirmed blockages needing catheter evaluation or treatment, heart attack care, and structural procedures. |

## The general cardiologist in detail

A general cardiologist is the physician who figures out what is wrong and keeps it under control. That work is mostly conversation, examination, and interpretation, and it is where most cardiac outcomes are actually determined.

The tools are largely non invasive. An [echocardiogram](/services/echocardiogram) shows valves and pumping strength. An [EKG](/services/electrocardiogram-ecg-ekg) shows the electrical pattern. A [treadmill stress test](/services/treadmill-stress-test) shows how the heart behaves under exertion. [Holter monitoring](/services/holter-monitoring) captures rhythm over a full day. Advanced lipid testing refines cholesterol risk.

The therapies are largely medical. Statins, blood pressure medication, diabetes control, anticoagulation for [atrial fibrillation](/conditions/atrial-fibrillation), heart failure drugs, smoking cessation, and structured exercise change long term outcomes more than any single procedure does. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/heart-disease/about/index.html) and the [American Heart Association](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/consumer-healthcare/what-is-cardiovascular-disease) both emphasize that risk factor management is the core of cardiovascular care.

Cardiology also has other subspecialties. Electrophysiologists treat rhythm disorders with ablation and implanted devices. Heart failure specialists manage advanced pump failure. Imaging specialists focus on advanced scans.

## The interventional cardiologist in detail

Interventional cardiology is the procedural branch. After the standard cardiology fellowship, an interventional fellow trains specifically in catheter techniques and then sits a separate certifying examination.

The daily work includes diagnostic coronary angiography, [angioplasty](/services/angioplasty), [coronary stenting](/services/coronary-stenting), and emergency treatment of heart attacks. Access is through the wrist or the groin, compared on our [transradial vs transfemoral angiogram](/compare/transradial-vs-transfemoral-angiogram) page. Many interventional cardiologists also treat peripheral arteries and veins, and some perform structural heart procedures.

Two points are worth stating plainly. First, an interventional cardiologist still practices general cardiology. Prescribing statins and adjusting blood pressure medicine is part of the job, not a lesser task delegated elsewhere. Second, being able to perform a procedure is not a reason to have one. For stable coronary disease, medication often performs as well as stenting for preventing heart attacks, a point covered on our [stent vs medication](/compare/stent-vs-medication) page. A good interventionalist says no often.

Cardiac surgery is a separate specialty entirely. Bypass operations are performed by cardiac surgeons, and interventional cardiologists refer and coordinate when surgery is the better answer. See [angioplasty vs bypass surgery](/compare/angioplasty-vs-bypass-surgery).

## How Dr. Nanavati decides which is right for you

In practice, patients rarely have to choose. Referral usually comes from a primary care physician, and the cardiologist decides what the case requires.

Symptoms set the direction. Stable exertional chest pain, shortness of breath, palpitations, or an abnormal EKG start with a general cardiac evaluation and non invasive testing.

Test results then determine escalation. A strongly abnormal stress test, imaging showing significant [coronary artery disease](/conditions/coronary-artery-disease), or a rising cardiac blood test moves the case toward catheter evaluation.

Urgency overrides everything. Chest pain at rest, or a suspected heart attack, means calling 911 and going to an emergency department, not booking an office appointment. Details are on our [chest pain](/conditions/chest-pain) page.

Continuity has real value. When one physician performs the diagnostic testing, reviews the angiogram, does the procedure if one is needed, and then manages medication afterward, less information is lost between handoffs.

Complexity sometimes needs a wider team. A cardiac surgeon, an electrophysiologist, or a vascular specialist may be the right person, and a good cardiologist says so. Background reading on heart conditions is available from [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/heartdiseases.html).

## Questions to ask your cardiologist

- What are your board certifications, and when were they most recently maintained?
- Will you manage my medications yourself, or hand that back to my primary care physician?
- If I need a procedure, will you perform it, and where?
- If my case needs surgery or an electrophysiologist, how does that referral work?
- Who reviews my test results, and how quickly will I hear about them?
- What can be handled at your office, and what requires a hospital?
