## What is an arrhythmia?

An arrhythmia is an abnormal heart rhythm. Your heartbeat is set by an electrical signal that starts in the upper chambers of the heart and travels down to the lower chambers, telling the muscle when to squeeze. When that signal starts in the wrong place, moves along the wrong path or gets blocked, the beat becomes too fast, too slow or irregular. Doctors group arrhythmias by speed and origin. Tachycardia means a resting rate above 100 beats per minute. Bradycardia means a rate below 60. Premature beats are early extra beats that feel like a skip or a thud. Atrial fibrillation, the most common sustained arrhythmia, comes from the upper chambers, while ventricular rhythms come from the lower chambers and are more dangerous. Many arrhythmias are harmless. Others raise the risk of stroke, fainting or cardiac arrest, which is why the [National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/arrhythmias) advises having a persistent irregular rhythm evaluated.

## Symptoms to watch for

Some arrhythmias produce no symptoms at all and turn up on a routine tracing. When they are felt, patients describe them in a few consistent ways.

Call the office to be seen if you have:

- Fluttering, pounding, racing or skipping beats that keep coming back
- A pulse that feels persistently fast or unusually slow
- Shortness of breath with ordinary activity
- Unusual fatigue, lightheadedness or a brief woozy spell
- A racing heartbeat that starts and stops abruptly

Call 911 if you have:

- Chest pain or pressure along with a racing or irregular heartbeat
- Fainting, or a near faint where you lost awareness
- Severe shortness of breath at rest
- Sudden weakness on one side, trouble speaking or a drooping face, which are [stroke](https://www.cdc.gov/stroke/) warning signs

If someone collapses and is not breathing normally, call 911 and start CPR. The [American Heart Association](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/cardiac-arrest) notes that immediate CPR can double or triple survival from cardiac arrest.

## How Dr. Nanavati diagnoses it

Rhythm problems are intermittent, so the goal is to record your heart during a symptom. Dr. Nanavati selects the monitor that matches how often your symptoms occur.

- **[Electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG)](/services/electrocardiogram-ecg-ekg)** takes about ten seconds and captures the rhythm at that moment. It is done in the office at the first visit.
- **[Holter monitoring](/services/holter-monitoring)** records every beat for 24 to 48 hours, which suits daily symptoms.
- **[7 to 10 day ambulatory rhythm monitoring](/services/7-10-day-ambulatory-rhythm-monitor)** covers symptoms that come only a few times a week.
- **[Loop recorder implantation and interrogation](/services/loop-recorder-implantation-and-interrogation)** uses a small device under the skin for rare events or unexplained fainting, and can watch for years.
- **[Echocardiogram](/services/echocardiogram)** shows the pumping strength and valves, since a weak or thickened heart muscle can drive arrhythmias.
- **[Treadmill stress testing](/services/treadmill-stress-test)** reveals rhythms that appear only with exertion, and [advanced stress testing](/services/advanced-stress-testing) is used when a plain treadmill study is not enough.
- **[Pacemaker interrogation](/services/pacemaker-interrogation)** and **[defibrillator interrogation](/services/defibrillator-interrogation)** download stored rhythm data if you already have a device.
- **[Advanced lipid testing](/services/advanced-lipid-testing)** and blood work help identify contributing causes such as thyroid or cholesterol problems.

## Treatment options

Treatment depends on the type of arrhythmia, your symptoms and your stroke risk. Many patients need only the first step.

**Lifestyle.** Caffeine, alcohol, stimulants, decongestants and poor sleep all trigger extra beats. Treating sleep apnea and untreated [high blood pressure](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure) often reduces episodes on its own. The habits in [Life's Essential 8](https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-lifestyle/lifes-essential-8) apply here directly.

**Medication.** Beta blockers and calcium channel blockers slow a fast rate. Antiarrhythmic drugs help hold a normal rhythm. If you have [atrial fibrillation](/conditions/atrial-fibrillation), an anticoagulant may be recommended to lower stroke risk, following current [American College of Cardiology guidelines](https://www.acc.org/Guidelines).

**Procedures.** Cardioversion resets a fast rhythm. Catheter ablation treats the tissue causing it. A pacemaker corrects a rhythm that is too slow, and an implantable defibrillator protects against dangerous [ventricular arrhythmias](/conditions/ventricular-arrhythmias). A [temporary pacemaker](/services/temporary-pacemaker) may be used in urgent situations. For patients with atrial fibrillation who cannot stay on blood thinners, [left atrial appendage closure](/services/left-atrial-appendage-closure) is an alternative. Procedures are performed at affiliated hospitals, with your evaluation and follow up handled in the office.

## What to expect at your visit

Bring your medication list, including supplements and over the counter cold remedies. Note when your episodes happen, how long they last and what you were doing. Recordings from a smart watch are useful, so bring them. Your visit includes an exam, a blood pressure check and an ECG. If a monitor is ordered, staff place it before you leave and explain how to mark symptoms. When the recording is read, you get a plain language explanation of what your heart was doing and what happens next. Common questions are answered on the [FAQs page](/faqs).

## When to see a cardiologist

See a cardiologist for repeated [palpitations](/conditions/heart-palpitations), a persistently irregular pulse, unexplained fainting, or palpitations with [chest pain](/conditions/chest-pain) or breathlessness.
